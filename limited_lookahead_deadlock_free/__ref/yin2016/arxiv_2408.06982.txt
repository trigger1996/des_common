                                                                     1. Introduction[eess.SY]
                  1.1. Motivation. Complex cyber-physical systems (CPSs), including intelligent transportation systems and
              manufacturing systems, are fundamental to safety-critical infrastructure. Nevertheless, these systems often
               experience failures that are challenging to detect due to the multitude of internal components and subsystems
               operating concurrently. This complexity renders fault diagnosis and detection essential yet challenging tasks,
             which are crucial for ensuring safety and functionality over complex CPSs.

            To tackle these challenges, assessing the diagnosability of complex CPSs is pivotal. Diagnosability, which
             was first introduced in [1], is a type of information-flow properties that determines whether or not faults
              within a system can be conclusively identified within a finite time frame after their occurrences. Analyzing
                diagnosability provides several advantages [2].  For example, in the design phase of CPSs, results from
                diagnosability analysis help optimizing sensor placement and the development of built-in tests, enhancing
                 later fault diagnosis and detection. For operational systems, these results inform improvements in diagnostics
             and maintenance scheduling. Therefore, a deeper understanding on the diagnosability of CPSs allows for a
                 cost-effective assessment of trade-offs among reliability, functionality, and diagnosability.

               Building upon the foundational study in [1], the field of diagnosability analysis has been extensively investigated,arXiv:2408.06982v1
                 especially within the context of discrete event systems (DES), which are characterized by event-driven dynamics
             and a discrete set of states, including Petri nets [3–9], stochastic automata [10–12], and finite state automata
                [13–15]. Note that these works mainly focus on exploring diagnosability utilizing event-based observation
              models, whose outputs are symbolically distinct and tagged with unique labels. However, in many real-world
                 applications, system outputs are typically continuous-space physical signals rather than discrete event sequences.
            Due to the interference of measurement noise in these signals, identifying two continuous signals with absolute
              accuracy are difficult, making it challenging to apply the traditional framework of diagnosability devised for
          DES to systems with continuous-space outputs.

            To bridge this gap, research efforts have expanded the concept of diagnosability to encompass systems operating
                in continuous state sets [16–19]. Specifically, [19] explored diagnosability in hybrid systems that exhibit both

continuous and discrete dynamics by representing mode switches as discrete events. The authors in [17]
introduced and verified a new notion of diagnosability for hybrid systems by abstracting them into timed
automata. This method was further refined in [18], which incorporated assessments of the impacts of time
delays and measurement uncertainties on fault detection. Additionally, [16] proposed the concept of approximate
diagnosability for metric systems, accounting for inprecision in output measurements. The assessment of this
approximate diagnosability was then conducted using a finite-state model, established through an approximate
simulation relation [16]. However, this method faces significant challenges due to the curse of dimensionality,
particularly because it requires discretizing the continuous state and input sets to build a finite abstraction of
the system.


1.2. Contribution. In this paper, we explore the concept of δ-approximate K-diagnosability in the context of
discrete-time control systems, extending the notion of K-diagnosability, as introduced in [20], to metric systems.
This requires that all faults should be detectable within a maximum of K time steps after they occur. As we
do not have any prior knowledge of whether or when faults will occur, the primary challenge in abstraction-free
verification lies in accurately tracking faults and recording the time elapsed since their occurrence without
relying on a finite abstraction of the original system.

To address these challenges, we construct a deterministic finite automaton ((δ, K)-DFA) that effectively
monitors fault occurrences and the elapsed time since their occurrence, independent of when the fault occurs.
Subsequently, through integrating this automaton with an augmented version of the discrete-time control
system to construct a product system, we establish the necessary and sufficient conditions under which the
control system achieves δ-approximate K-diagnosability. Note that these conditions also provide a uniform
framework for verifying (the lack of) δ-approximate K-diagnosability over both discrete-event systems and
continuous-space systems, regardless of using discretization-based or discretization-free approaches. Based
on these conditions, the verification of (the lack of) δ-approximate K-diagnosability is refined into a safety
verification problem over the product system, which is essentially a hybrid system. To solve such a safety
verification problem, we leverage the notion of hybrid barrier certificates introduced in [21] and propose two
methodologies for computing these certificates, grounded in the counterexample-guided inductive synthesis
(CEGIS) framework and sum-of-squares (SOS) programming. Additionally, we propose the construction of a
diagnoser capable of detecting faults when the system is shown to be δ-approximate K-diagnosable.

A limited subset of the results in this paper has been presented in [22]. Here, we provide detailed proofs of
the results in [22] that were omitted. Apart from the verification of diagnosability, here, we also propose
methodologies to verify the lack of δ-approximate K-diagnosability via hybrid barrier certificates. Accordingly,
we propose two computational schemes, namely CEGIS-based and SOS-based schemes, to compute hybrid
barrier certificates to verify (the lack of) diagnosability. Furthermore, construction of a diagnoser that detects
fault is provided, which was missing in [22].


1.3. Related Works. Since diagnosability is a class of information-flow properties, it is important to highlight
various different approximate notions of information-flow properties. For example, to enhance security measures,
the concept of approximate opacity was proposed for metric systems in [23] and subsequently extended for
discrete-time stochastic systems in [24]. Focusing on state estimation, approximate observability was investigated
for discrete-time nonlinear systems in [25]. These results typically require the construction of finite abstractions
from the original systems, which can lead to scalability issues due to the discretization of continuous state and
input sets. To overcome these limitations, discretization-free methods were introduced in [26,27], which employ
barrier certificates to ascertain approximate opacity. It is crucial to note that these methodologies essentially
address different information-flow properties than those discussed in this paper.

Additionally, we would also like to mention the results in [28], which showed that diagnosability over DES can be
formulated as HyperLTL properties by proper system transformation over the original DES, and in [29], which
focused on a class of HyperLTL property called conditional invariance. They showed that the verification of
such properties can be solved by exploiting the automata-based structures associated with the complements of

these properties. However, δ-approximate K-diagnosability does not fall into the class of conditional invariance.
Moreover, it is still an open problem for how to perform the system transformation introduced in [28] over

the best of our knowledge, the current work as well as its preliminary version [22] are the first to propose

1.4. Organization. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.  Section 2 introduces notations,
foundational models, and problem formulation of this paper.  Section 3 details the construction of a (δ,

In Section 5, we describe the construction of a diagnoser based on the system satisfying the δ-approximate

2.1. Notations. We denote by R and N the set of real numbers and non-negative integers, respectively. These
symbols are annotated with subscripts to restrict them in the usual way, e.g., R>0 denotes the set of positive
real numbers. For a, b ∈R (resp. a, b ∈N) with a ≤b, the closed, open and half-open intervals in R (resp.
N) are denoted by [a, b], (a, b), [a, b), and (a, b], respectively. Moreover, Rn×m, with n, m ∈N≥1, denotes the
vector space of real matrices with n rows and m columns. Given N ∈N≥1 vectors xi ∈Rni, with i ∈[1; N],
ni ∈N≥1, and n=Pi ni, we denote the concatenated vector in Rn by x=[x1;. . .; xN] and the Euclidean norm
of x by ∥x∥. Given a set X, we denote by 2X the power set of X. Given a matrix A, we denote by A⊤
and Ar(i) the transpose and the i-th row of A, respectively. Considering a set Y , Y ∗denotes the Cartesian
product of a finite number of Y . Given sets X and Y , the complement of X with respect to Y  is defined
as Y \ X = {x ∈Y  : x /∈X}. Given functions f : X →Y and g : A →B, we define f × g : X × A →Y × B as
(f × g)(x, a) = (f(x), g(a)).


2.2. Preliminaries. In this paper, we focus on discrete-time control systems defined as below.

Definition 2.1. A discrete-time control system (dt-CS) is a tuple Σ := (X, X0, XF , U, f, Y, h), where X ⊂Rn,
X0 ⊆X, XF ⊂X, U ⊂Rm, and Y ⊂Rq denote the state set, initial state set, faulty state set, input set, and
output set, respectively, which are bounded. Moreover, the function f : X × U →X and h : X →Y are the
state transition function and the output function, respectively.


Equivalently, a dt-CS Σ can be described by

                                   x(k + 1) = f(x(k), ν(k)),
                    Σ :                                                                         (2.1)                                         y(k) = h(x(k)),      k ∈N,

where x(k) ∈X, ν(k) ∈U, and y(k) ∈Y are the state, input, and output of the system at time step k, respec-
tively. Additionally, we denote by ν := (ν(0), . . . , ν(k), . . .) an input run of Σ, by xx0,ν := (x(0), . . . , x(k), . . .)
a state run of Σ starting from initial state x0 under input run ν, i.e., x(0) = x0, x(k + 1) = f(x(k), ν(k)),
∀k ∈N, and by yx0,ν the output run corresponding to xx0,ν.

A state run xx0,ν is deemed a faulty run if it visits the set of faulty states XF ⊂X, which means there exists
i ∈N such that xx0,ν(i) ∈XF . Here, we say that a fault occurs if the system’s run visits the faulty state set
for the first time. It is important to note that XF can represent either a physically unsafe region or a region
where a task violation is detected. In the interest of safety, it becomes imperative to diagnose the occurrence
of a fault in a timely manner, preventing the possibility of cascading disasters. It is worth mentioning that the

repair of faults is not considered in this work. In other words, once the system enters a faulty state, it remains
faulty indefinitely, even if it subsequently exits the faulty state set.

In most cases, the output run cannot be measured perfectly due to measurement noises. Therefore, we assume
that the output run can only be observed imprecisely. Considering the actual output run yx0,ν, one has
                                         ||yx0,ν(k) −yδx0,ν(k)|| ≤δ, ∀k ∈N,                                   (2.2)
in which δ ∈R≥0 represents the observation precision and yδx0,ν is the actual observation associated with yx0,ν
that is available. Given an observation precision δ, a fundamental question is whether or not faults in the
dt-CS as in Definition 2.1 can always be diagnosed. This leads to the notion of δ-approximate K-diagnosability,
which is formally defined below.
Definition 2.2. Consider a dt-CS Σ = (X, X0, XF , U, f, Y, h). Given a constant δ ∈R>0, and K ∈N, Σ is
said to be δ-approximate K-diagnosable, if for all xx0,v := (x0, . . . , xk, . . .) such that x0 ∈X0, xk ∈XF , and
xi /∈XF , ∀i ∈[0, k −1]; and for all ˆxˆx0,ˆv := (ˆx0, . . . , ˆxk, . . .) such that ˆx0 ∈X0, and ˆxi /∈XF , ∀i ∈[0, k + K],
one has maxi∈[0,k+K] ||h(xi) −h(ˆxi)|| > δ.

Intuitively, K ∈N represents the maximal time window following a fault occurrence at time k, within which
the fault must be diagnosed. To verify whether or not a dt-CS is δ-approximate K-diagnosable, the notion of
deterministic finite automata [30] is leveraged, which is introduced below.

Definition 2.3. (DFA) A deterministic finite automata (DFA) is a tuple A =(Q, q0, Π, τ, F), in which Q
is a finite set of states, q0 ∈Q is the initial state, Π is a finite set of alphabet, τ : Q × Π →Q is a transition
function, and F ⊆Q is a set of accepting states.

Considering the transition function τ, we further define a function Nxt : Q→2Q by:
                               Nxt(q):={q′ ∈Q : ∃σ∈Π, q′ =τ(q, σ)}.                                 (2.3)

Additionally, a notion of labeling function is required to connect a dt-CS to a given DFA A.

Definition 2.4. (Labeling Function) Consider a dt-CS Σ := (X, X0, XF , U, f, Y, h), a DFA A=(Q, q0, Π, τ,
F), and a finite state run xx0,ν := (x(0), . . . , x(H)) ∈XH+1 of Σ with H ∈N>0 under an input run ν :=
(ν(0), . . . , ν(H −1)). We define a labeling function L : X →Π and a function LH : XH+1 →ΠH+1 such that
the trace of xx0,ν over Π is σ :=LH(xx0,ν)= (σ0, σ1, . . . , σH) with σk = L(xx0,ν(k)) for all k ∈[0, H].

2.3. Main Problem. In this paper, our primary focus is on the verification of δ-approximate K-diagnosability
over a dt-CS. When the dt-CS is diagnosable, we also aim to construct a diagnoser capable of detecting a fault
within a maximum of K time steps after its initial occurrence, without indicating a fault if none has occurred.
This diagnoser, referred to as a (δ,K)-diagnoser, is formally defined below.
Definition 2.5. ((δ,K)-diagnoser) Consider a dt-CS Σ := (X, X0, XF , U, f, Y, h), constants δ ∈R≥0 and
K ∈N. A (δ,K)-diagnoser is a function
                         D : Y ∗→{0, 1},                                            (2.4)
satisfying the following conditions:

     • (C1) For all k ∈N and xx0,v such that xx0,v(i) /∈XF , ∀i ∈[0, k −1], and xx0,v(k) ∈XF , one has
      D(yδx0,ν(0), . . . , yδx0,ν(k + K)) = 1;
     • (C2) For all xx0,v such that ∀i ∈N, xx0,v(i) /∈XF , one has D(yδxo,ν) = 0,

where yδx0,ν ∈Y ∗, with yδx0,ν being an imprecise observation defined as in (2.2).

3.1. Verification via (δ, K)-Deterministic Finite Automata. Consider a dt-CS Σ=(X, X0, XF , U, f, Y, h).
To tackle Problem 2.6, we introduce an augmented system associated with Σ, which is the product between Σ
and itself, defined as

                    Σaug := (X × X, X0 × X0, XF × XF , U × U, f × f, Y × Y, h × h).                   (3.1)

Here, we denote by (x, ˆx)∈X×X a state pair of Σ×Σ, and by (xx0,ν, ˆxˆx0,ˆν) the state run of Σ × Σ starting
from (x0, ˆx0) under input run (ν, ˆν). Moreover, we use R=X× X to represent the augmented state set. Then,
considering the desired δ-approximate K-diagnosability property, we propose the construction of a so-called
(δ,K)-DFA over the augmented system Σaug, which plays a key role in our main results for the verification over
the original dt-CS Σ against the desired diagnosability property.

Definition 3.1. ((δ,K)-DFA) Consider a dt-CS Σ := (X, X0, XF , U, f, Y, h), the corresponding augmented
system Σaug as in (3.1), and constants δ ∈R>0, K ∈N. Consider sets P1 := {(x, ˆx) : ||h(x) −h(ˆx)|| ≤δ},
P2 := {(x, ˆx) : x ∈XF , ˆx ∈X}, and P3 := {(x, ˆx) : ˆx ∈XF , x ∈X}, with P1, P2, P3 ⊆R. We define a

     • State set ¯Q := {¯q0, q1, . . . , qK, ¯F, qtrap}.
     • Alphabet ¯Π := {σ1, σ2, σ3}, with ¯L being defined such that ¯L−1(σ1) := P1 ∩(R \ P2) ∩(R \ P3),

     • Transition function ¯τ is defined such that q0 = ¯τ(q0, σ1); q1 = ¯τ(q0, σ3); qtrap = ¯τ(qi, σ2), ∀i ∈[0, K];

3.2. Verifying (the Lack of) Approximate K-Diagnosability. In the previous subsection, we reformulated
the verification of (the lack of) diagnosability as a safety verification problem over the product system
Σaug ⊗A(δ,K) in (3.2). To solve this safety verification problem, in this subsection, we deploy a notion of hybrid
barrier certificates (HBC) over the hybrid state set of Σaug ⊗A(δ,K). The notion of hybrid barrier certificates
was introduced for the first time in [21], but in a different context. First, we propose the following result related
to the verification of δ-approximate K-diagnosability.

                              4. Computation of Hybrid Barrier Certificates

In this section, we propose two methodologies to compute B-HBC and V-HBC as introduced in Theorems 3.5
and 3.6, respectively. The first methodology deploys a CEGIS framework [31], under which the HBCs are
computed using Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) and Gurobi [32] solvers. As the second approach, we
cast the computation of the hybrid barrier certificates as a sum-of-squares (SOS) programming that can be
solved using semi-definite-programming (SDP) solvers (e.g., Mosek [33]). Note that the notions of monomial,
polynomial, and SOS polynomials will be deployed when introducing the SOS-based computation approaches.
Here, we refer readers to [34] for the definitions of these notions, and we omit them here due to the lack of
space.

                                       5. Designing Diagnosers for dt-CS

 In this section, we discuss how to design a diagnoser to detect the occurrence of fault if the dt-CS is shown to
 be δ-approximate K-diagnosable by leveraging the results in Sections 3 and 4. First, we propose Theorem 5.1,

 being δ-approximate K-diagnosable.

                                                    7. Conclusion

In this paper, we developed an abstraction-free, automata-based framework for verifying δ-approximate K-
diagnosability using hybrid barrier certificates (HBC) over discrete-time control systems with both continuous
and discrete states and input sets. Initially, we designed a (δ,K)-DFA that tracks the number of time steps
following fault occurrences. This allows the verification of (the lack of) δ-approximate K-diagnosability to
be recasted as a safety verification problem over a product system that integrates the (δ,K)-DFA with an
augmented version of the discrete-time control system. We further show that the safety verification problem
over the product system can be addressed by computing appropriate HBCs. Accordingly, we introduced
two methodologies for computing HBCs, utilizing sum-of-squares programming and counter-example-guided
inductive synthesis. Moreover, we propose the construction of a diagnoser to identify faults in systems that are
δ-approximate K-diagnosable. Our future work aims to extend the current result to fault models described by
language patterns [40,41] or temporal logics [42,43].
